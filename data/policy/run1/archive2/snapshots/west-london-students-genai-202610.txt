Using AI in your studies

AI refers to the theory and development of computer systems capable of performing tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as visual perception, speech recognition, decision-making, and translation between languages.

Generative AI is artificial intelligence that can create content, such as text, images, video or audio in response to a user’s prompt or request. It relies on sophisticated machine learning models called deep learning models, which simulate the learning and decision-making processes of the human brain.

Generative AI may be used as a tool, resource, or consultant during your studies but not as a replacement for your own knowledge, critical thinking, reasoning or self-reflection.

Overusing AI might potentially weaken critical thinking, reduce research skills and encourage shallow learning as well as shortcuts to the learning process. Becoming overly dependent on AI tools can potentially harm motivation, effort and academic integrity.

You should always acknowledge the use of AI, just like any other source, and reference it correctly.

-

Planning

- AI can provide a plan or structure for your assignment, project or research.
- Help manage your time by creating a weekly timetable for you.
- It can also remind you of tasks to be completed and encourage you to make a start.

-

Finding information

- AI tools can be used to create keywords or phrases to search for information in other tools.
- It can help find information sources by following the citations provided by the AI tool.

-

Understanding information

-
AI can explain difficult concepts to make learning easier for you.

-
It can summarise chunks of texts, including journal articles.

-
Help you with data processing, analysis and visualisation.

-
Change the format of information to a way you prefer, whether as a summary report or table or graph.

-
Help create flowcharts and timelines.

-

Writing your assignment

- AI can provide you with images for your assessments.
- Help you to improve your grammar and writing style.
- Provide feedback on your own writing.

-

Understanding your learning

- AI can summarise a transcript of your lecture to help you discover the key points, when you have permission to do this.
- Test your knowledge of the topic by creating a test or quiz.
- Provide examples, case studies and practical tips to understand the scenario in a different way.
- Give you insights into your ideas and facilitate a virtual discussion. Since the AI tool understands natural language and single word prompts, this stimulates a classroom friend.

-

Understanding your feedback

- Use AI tools to provide feedback on practice exams or to generate action plans to address areas for improvement.
- Use this feedback to adjust your study plan, but rely on your own judgment and understanding for complex or nuanced topics.

How not to use AI and AI tools

- AI shouldn’t be used as your only source of information in your studies and for your assignments. You need to access a variety of sources to get a comprehensive view of your topic.
- It must not be used to write or create your coursework or assignments for you. This would be considered academic misconduct as described in UWL’s academic offences regulations. Your coursework or assignment must be your own work, and any work which is not your own must be referenced correctly.
- It must not be used to replace your own critical thinking. Your work needs to reflect your evaluation of the evidence and your thoughts.
- You must keep within the guidelines for using AI and AI tools which are set out in your assessment brief.
- You should never rely on AI as a substitute for reading research, official sources of law or regulatory codes. Information provided by AI must be cross-checked against these sources.

How to reference AI tools and AI generated content

If you do decide to use an AI tool as a source of information in your course work or assignment, you must reference it correctly. When incorporating AI-generated content into your own work, you must acknowledge it by providing correct references. If you fail to do so this may result in academic misconduct.

Find out how to reference AI tools at Cite Them Right - Generative AI page.

If an AI source has directed you to another webpage or a document and it is information on this page or in this document you are using in your course work or assignment, it is the webpage or the document you need to reference. In this case, the AI source is acting as a search tool, directing you to another source.

What are the aspects to be kept in mind when using Generative AI?

Like any source of information, AI should be viewed critically and evaluated to ensure it is relevant, of high quality and backed by evidence. Investigate to see if the information you have read or been told is supported by other sources, and always check the evidence.

-

Bias

Generative AI models are trained on data created by people, therefore may reproduce biases in gender, religion, race, nationality, age, ability, and history. For example, describing nurses as women and engineers as men. Generative AI companies founded in the USA favour American English language and cultural norms.

-

Simplification

They can present highly contentious or complicated issues in over simplified or one-sided ways.

-

Out of date content

Depending on how the AI tool has been trained, how it accesses information and the version you are using, it may not contain up to date content.

-

Misinformation

Text, media and images generated by AI tools can spread misinformation.

-

Quality

You won’t know what the AI tool has access to or been trained on so you may find the information being provided by the tool is limited or of low quality.

-

Citations

Not all tools will link to the sources it has used to create your answer, which makes it harder for you to follow the evidence to evaluate it.

-

Hallucinations

If the AI tool is programmed to give you an answer regardless of whether the information is available to it, it will make up information to answer the question. These responses are called hallucinations. You may be able to find a more truthful answer by further prompting.

-

Hallucinations of citations

An AI tool may also provide you with hallucinations of citations, providing a reference to a journal article, law case or other format which doesn’t exist. Always check the citation details to check they are real and reliable.

-

Data privacy

This means how user data is collected, stored, and used. To ensure that personal information remains secure and protected, UWL recommends you use Copilot.

Using AI in your assessment

It is expected that academic integrity is upheld in all assessed work. You must never submit work generated by an AI tool and present it as your own.

Using an AI tool to gain an unfair advantage by misrepresenting its work as your own is academic misconduct and can have serious consequences.

The traffic light system

The traffic light system

The use of AI tools in assessments follows the traffic light system. Each assessment is marked as ‘Red’, ‘Amber’ or ‘Green’ and this tells you how you can use AI in that assignment.

You will find the details of AI tools and functionality suitable to your assignment in your assessment brief and your lecturer will provide examples of how these can or cannot be used in the assignment.

Your lecturers will ensure that these meet the requirements of professional bodies that accredit some of our courses.

-

Red category

The use of Gen AI tools (eg ChatGPT, DeepSeek, etc) is not permitted.

The purpose and format of the assessments makes it inappropriate or impractical for AI tools to be used.

-

Amber category

We encourage you to use Generative AI tools to help you with preparing this assessment.

Always check your Assessment Brief, where you will find the details of AI tools and functionality suitable to your assignment and examples of how these can or cannot be used in the assignment.

-

Green category

Using Generative AI tools effectively is part of this assessment.

Always check your Assessment Brief for details specific to your assignment.

-

Academic integrity

Behaving ethically, fairly, honestly, responsibly, and transparently when studying or undertaking and reporting research is essential. This includes but is not limited to:

- Acknowledging sources (not plagiarising).
- Originality and authorship (only submit work and results that are your own).
- Ownership (not uploading your work to non-UWL tools or giving it to other students).

-

Academic misconduct

Submitting work that does not have Academic Integrity: the most common form of academic misconduct is plagiarism. It can also include using ghostwriters (including AI tools), research misconduct, and exam misconduct, as well as all other kinds of cheating to gain an unfair academic advantage.

AI and environment

Using AI mindfully with consideration of its environmental impact

UWL is committed to reducing its environmental impact, as set out in our Environmental policy. Generative AI tools are powered by energy and water intensive Data Processing centres. Projected AI energy use may account for 30% of total world energy consumption by 2030, as suggested by Bolin-Canedo et al. Similar concerns have been discussed by numerous researchers from a range of disciplines (Stahl and Eke, 2024; Ren and Wierman, 2024).

We encourage students are encouraged to use AI tools mindfully due to their environmental impact, in a similar way to the mindful use of paper and printing. Top tips for sustainable use include:

-
Turn off embedded AI tools within search engines when using search engines for simple searches.

-
Create a clear and specific prompt engineering search beforehand to reduce the number of AI searches you run to obtain information.

- When reading journal articles, read the summary or abstract at the start of the article rather than using AI to create a summary.

Key terminology (FAQs)

-

What is AI?

AI refers to the theory and development of computer systems capable of performing tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as visual perception, speech recognition, decision-making, and translation between languages. AI can also be referred to as machine intelligence, although this term is slightly broader.

-

What is Generative AI (Gen AI)?

Generative AI is artificial intelligence that can create content, such as text, images, video or audio in response to a user’s prompt or request. It relies on sophisticated machine learning models called deep learning models, which simulate the learning and decision-making processes of the human brain.

Some examples of generative AI tools are Copilot, ChatGPT and Adobe Firefly. The University of West London has opted to use Microsoft Copilot and Adobe Express as its base AI technologies.

Microsoft Copilot is an advanced AI-powered tool integrated into various Microsoft applications, designed to assist users by providing contextual suggestions and automating repetitive tasks. It serves as a virtual assistant, guiding users through complex processes and enabling more focused and creative work. It analyses patterns in language, process context, and responds in a way that feels natural and engaging. Unlike traditional AI that retrieves information, Copilot generates unique responses based on the training and real-time understanding.

-

What does it mean when people say use AI ethically?

AI tools can support your learning when used ethically. What this means is:

- Not claiming work created by the AI tool is your own.
- Not processing personal or confidential information. For example, uncoded responses from a research survey.
- Not uploading someone else’s content to an AI tool where you don’t have permission to do this. This includes published sources as well as information you may have from live case studies used in your classes and client information you may be working with.
- Knowing what the AI tool is going to do with the information you provide to it.
- Being aware of the content in the previous section.
- Not making decisions based on AI generated content without reviewing it.

-

What is prompt engineering, and what is a prompt?

To use AI effectively, you need to ask the right questions. This is known as prompt engineering and is the practice of crafting clear, specific, and purposeful prompts to guide the AI’s response.

Here are some tips:

- Be specific. Instead of asking “tell me about climate change,” try “what are the main causes of climate change according to recent scientific literature?”
- Set context. Include the academic level or subject area. For example, “explain the concept of opportunity cost in economics for a first-year university student” or “Explain Donoghue v Stevenson in simple terms suitable for a first-year law student.”
- Request structure. Ask for bullet points, summaries, comparisons or definitions to suit your needs.
- Ask for sources: You can request references but always check them yourself.

This approach helps AI provide more focused and useful responses, supporting better academic research and learning.

-

How do I know what prompt to use

There are several frameworks that explain how prompts can be constructed in an effective way to get the best possible answer from an AI tool. The RAIR framework is one of them. RAIR relates to structured guidance, particularly in areas like AI, programming, or workflows.

Role. Begin by telling the AI tool that enables it to adopt a persona eg “I am a first-year undergraduate student”, “you are a London theatre critic”, “I am a pastry chef.” Be aware that the AI tool remembers, so don’t forget about data privacy.

Action. Specify the task or activity eg “explain to me the concept of…”, “look through the synopsis of my play”, “translate this French recipe from 1890.”

Instructions. These are what the AI should produce eg “create bullet point list”, “write a two-sentence review of this”, “use metric system and Celsius for measurements and temperature.”

Requirements. These are the essential elements needed to complete your task. If you're researching, requirements might include the AI tool uses academic journals and proper citation methods.

-

What is meant by data privacy in an AI tool?

Data privacy in an AI tool refers to how user data is collected, stored, and utilised, ensuring that personal information remains secure and protected. It covers principles like confidentiality, consent, and security measures to prevent unauthorised access or misuse. To keep your data as private as possible, UWL recommends you use Copilot. Inputting confidential information into AI tools could breach GDPR and professional society ethics codes.

-

What are the environmental costs of generative AI?

Generative AI comes with significant environmental costs. Training large-scale models requires vast computational power, which in turn demands substantial electricity, resulting in significant carbon emissions. Data centres hosting these models also consume immense quantities of water for cooling, straining local resources (although ways of using this water are being investigated). Beyond the operational phase, the manufacture and disposal of specialised hardware contribute to e-waste and resource depletion. As usage scales globally, these impacts grow proportionally, prompting calls for greater transparency, efficiency and sustainability in AI development.

-

What are large language models and are they different to generative AI?

Large language models (LLMs) are advanced artificial intelligence systems trained on vast amounts of text data to understand and generate human-like language. They use deep learning techniques, specifically neural networks, to analyse patterns and context in text, enabling them to answer questions, engage in conversations, translate languages, and even create content. These models improve over time as they process more information, making interactions increasingly sophisticated and natural.

Large language models (LLMs) are a type of generative AI. Generative AI refers to any AI model that generates content rather than simply analysing data. LLMs specifically focus on processing and generating human-like text, making them a specialised subset of generative AI. Other examples of generative AI include models that create realistic images or compose music, so while all LLMs are generative AI, not all generative AI models are LLMs.

-

Top tips for using AI

- Use AI for planning, idea generation, clarification, and drafting.
- Always evaluate the content found.
- Treat AI as a study aid, not an authority.
- Reference any AI generated content you are using in your assessment.
- Keep your data secure.
- Follow the guidance your lecturer provides about AI usage for your assessments, following the traffic light system.

How can I get further help?

Students should make use of formative assessments, Subject Librarians, the Study Support team, and the academic experts at their school or college to practice and develop their AI knowledge.
