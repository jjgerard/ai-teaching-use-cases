What to know about using AI while studying at LSE

0 comments

Estimated reading time: 10 minutes

AI is entering every area of our lives and academia is no exception. But are you actually familiar with the current AI approach at LSE? This post gives an overview of the rules, resources and recommendations for using AI while studying at LSE. One important disclaimer: this was written in May 2026, and the technology is evolving fast: Please double-check LSE’s webpages for the latest updates.

Rules: the AI policy

LSE encourages AI fluency rather than prohibiting use, but the detail matters. Tools for grammar and spell check (Grammarly, DeepL, Microsoft Word’s built-in editor) are generally fine. However, each department sets out its own policy for assessments, whether they allow no, limited or full authorised use of generative AI. Your first step should always be to check your home department’s guidance.

My department, the European Institute, for example, allows generative AI to be used to support learning and research. This includes identifying articles, directing further reading, or explaining unfamiliar terms, much like a database search. But summative and formative assessments must be planned, researched and written solely by the student: no AI wording, in original or amended form, and no AI outputs cited as sources. Passing off AI-generated work as your own counts as plagiarism.

Beyond the department’s rules, LSE’s broader research guidance (PDF) highlights further aspects of AI usage. These include data privacy, copyright, and the environmental impact.

Resources: building AI fluency

LSE has a useful set of AI resources. First of all, the university provides institutional access to Claude and Microsoft Copilot. Anthropic guarantees that data shared with Claude by LSE staff and students will not be used to train their models, which makes it safer to use compared to other AI chatbots.

Beyond providing access, there are ample resources to improve your AI literacy. Starting with the online ones: the Moodle course AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations is open to all students via self-enrolment and offers a certificate on completion. LSE LIFE also runs sessions on how to integrate AI into your studies. I personally went to “Use AI tools critically for effective academic reading” and “Use AI tools critically for effective academic writing“, both of which gave a good introduction and overview. If you miss them, the materials stay on Moodle.

My personal favourite was the Digital Skills Lab’s Generative AI Workshop. It went more in-depth, covering how LLMs actually work, why they hallucinate, and practical prompting techniques. Understanding the mechanics, that LLMs are stochastic and predict the next likely word rather than reasoning, changed how I use them. Read Mina’s blog to find out what else the Digital Skills Lab has to offer.

Recommendations: AI in your studies

Finally, I would like to share three tips on how I use AI in my student life.

Firstly, I use Claude for coding, especially for graphs in R. It is capable of designing complex graphs in seconds. In my Claude settings I defined my preferences, so regardless of which chat or project I am working in, it always applies the style I want.

Secondly, I use the project function in Claude to give it better context without overloading the chat capacity. I then ask it to explain concepts from the readings or quiz me on content. With the artifact function you can even build your own quiz apps with Claude.

Thirdly, through the LSE Financial Times subscription you also have access to the FT’s AI assistant Ask FT. Especially when searching for current examples this is a great tool, as it only draws on FT articles and so provides answers grounded in trusted journalism.

The bottom line

If you are in doubt, check with your department first. You can also book a one-to-one with LSE LIFE to ask a study adviser for help. Failing to capitalise on the efficiency gains AI offers puts you at a disadvantage in a world that’s adopting it fast. However, over-relying on it is just as risky, because then you’re the one who becomes replaceable. The interesting question is how to use it well.
