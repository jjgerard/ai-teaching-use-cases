Generative AI (GenAI)

AI is typically used to refer to all applications of AI methods, including generative AI (GenAI) and machine translation (MT).

AI is evolving rapidly, and it is not always clear when a tool uses AI. Grammarly is an example of a tool that uses AI, MT, and GenAI. For that reason, UQ refers to AI, but the UQ rules on AI are about how students use AI tools that generate content.

UQ’s AI rules are focused on content creation or assistance.

Artificial intelligence or AI is embedded into our digital world. It is designed to perform specific tasks based on set rules or data, like finding the fastest route or recognising faces. You likely use AI everyday, including for:

- grammar checkers – Grammarly, Microsoft Word’s editor tools

- spelling and autocorrect tools – built into word processors and browsers

- search engines – Google, Bing

- voice-to-text transcriptions – Otter.ai, Apple Dictation

- data analysis tools – Excel functions, SPSS, MATLAB

- recommendation algorithms (Netflix, Amazon).

What is Artificial Intelligence?

Machine translation or MT is an automated process in which a computer program converts text in one language into another. Examples of using MT:

- Converting an assessment you wrote from one language to another.

- Translating a website into another language to help you interpret it.

Many UQ students use Grammarly. It can directly translate text written in over 15 languages to English.

Generative AI or GenAI creates new information or outputs—like images, text, music—based on existing data. Examples of these tools include ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot.

Large language models

The technology behind generative AI is known as the Large language model (LLM). LLMs are 'fed' massive amounts of data which they use to train themselves to recognise and interpret human language and other types of data. LLMs don't truly comprehend meaning or intent like humans do, but they excel at pattern recognition and outputting predictions based on extremely large amounts of data.

Agentic AI

AI agents can perform tasks and complete workflows with minimal human oversight. They are becoming increasingly common in online customer support. Many AI tools now allow you to create your own agents to automate tasks and improve efficiency.

AI can sound very confident even when it’s wrong, so treat AI outputs as a starting point that you then need to evaluate and verify.

A few quick checks you can do:

- Cross-check key facts (names, dates, stats) against trusted sources like reputable journals, and official data (e.g. Australian Bureau of Statistics).

- Follow the evidence: open any citations or links the tool provides and make sure they actually support the claim and aren’t made up.

- Check the logic: do the steps make sense, and do the numbers add up?

- Watch for bias: look for one-sided framing, stereotypes, or emotionally loaded language.

- Check the tool, not just the answer: who made it, what is it drawing on, and what are the limits and privacy settings.

Online and on social media, AI-generated images and videos (deepfakes) can be used to mislead. Before you share or use media, try to find the original source or context, do a reverse image search, and cross-check the claim with reliable reporting or official statements.

Ethics and biases

While AI tools are very powerful, they have a range of limitations:

- GenAI models may produce inaccurate information.

- There may be no acknowledgement or reference to the source of information.

- The data used to train the models has biases and these biases can be amplified in GenAI models.

- AI tools are modelled to imitate aspects of intelligence, but they do not have the same capabilities as humans.

- AI suggested changes may change your intended meaning, even if only used for editing assistance.

Many UQ students are thinking deeply about how AI impacts learning.

2026 UQ student perspectives

Emerging insights from the 2026 Student Perspectives on AI in Higher Education survey from AIinHE.org indicate that university students continue to have concerns about using AI in their studies.

In the survey, the 5 strongest reasons why students limit or avoid AI are:

- It can be inaccurate.

- Personal learning values.

- I want to do it myself.

- Worried about breaking rules.

- Ethical concerns.

- Students recognise AI’s potential for significant impacts in their learning, future careers, and society broadly. They want UQ to support them to feel prepared and confident for the future with AI.

Using AI effectively for study vs cheating

What may or may not be an appropriate way to use AI to study or support you to complete an assessment task will depend on the course learning outcomes. If there is any doubt, check with your course coordinator.

Remember that if you use AI for an assessment task you must:

- be able to demonstrate your learning, independent of AI tools.

- appropriately acknowledge and reference your use of AI.

Examples of acceptable and unacceptable use of AI for study

Grammar and spelling

Use
Acceptable
Unacceptable

Grammar and spelling

You use Microsoft Word Editor or Grammarly to review spelling, grammar, and punctuation in your essay.

Why it’s OK* - this is considered an editing tool, similar to autocorrect, and doesn’t generate new content.

You use Grammarly to rewrite entire sentences or paragraphs without acknowledgment.

Why it’s not OK - it starts to shift from simple assistance to significant content contribution.

*In courses where the learning outcome is about you learning grammar or spelling, use editing tools outside of class to support your learning and to prepare you for secure assessment where you do not have access to such tools.

Brainstorm ideas

Use
Acceptable
Unacceptable
Unacceptable

Brainstorm ideas

You use ChatGPT or Microsoft Copilot to brainstorm ideas for an essay, like generating a list of potential topics or examples.

Why it’s OK* - this is comparable to a brainstorming session with peers or using a library resource, provided you do the actual writing.

You use GenAI tools to generate an entire essay outline or full paragraphs.

Why it’s not OK - the tool is now completing the intellectual work expected of you at UQ.

You brainstorm with friends, and all use ChatGPT together, then you copy the ideas from your friends and AI into your assessment.

Why it’s not OK – you are submitting work that is not your own, which is a form of plagiarism called collusion that is a violation of UQ’s academic integrity rules.

*In courses where the learning outcome is about generating ideas or brainstorming, use AI tools carefully when you study to ensure you are prepared for secure assessments where such tools are not allowed.

Analyse survey data

Use
Acceptable
Unacceptable

Analyse data

You use Excel or SPSS to analyse data for a project.

Why it’s OK* - these tools follow programmed instructions, and you are interpreting the results and calculations.

You use a GenAI tool to generate a complete data analysis and explanation/interpretation, submitting it as your own work.

Why it’s not OK - the analysis is no longer your own.

*In courses where the learning outcome is about writing the scripts, codes or formulas to command statistical software tools, use AI carefully when you study to ensure you are prepared for secure assessments when such tools are not available.

Create images or slides

Use
Acceptable
Unacceptable

Create images or slides

You use DALL&middot;E or PowerPoint Copilot to generate an image or slide background for a presentation, acknowledging its use.

Why it’s OK – the tool enhances visual appeal without contributing to the intellectual content.

You generate a diagram or infographic containing key ideas or data points but claim it as your own work.

Why it’s not OK - this constitutes an unacknowledged contribution of intellectual content.

Exam preparation

Use
Acceptable
Unacceptable

Create practice questions

You ask Copilot or ChatGPT to generate practice quiz questions based on the week’s lecture topics (or your own notes), then you answer them yourself and check your work.

Why it’s OK – it’s like making flashcards: a study aid.

You use AI to generate answers for graded quizzes/tutorial submissions, or you copy AI answers into anything you submit.

Why it’s not OK – the tool is doing the assessed work, and if not acknowledged it can be academic misconduct.

Translation or language support

Use
Acceptable
Unacceptable

Translation for understanding

You translate a tricky sentence/paragraph from a reading to help you understand it, then you paraphrase the meaning in your own words in your notes.

Why it’s OK* – it’s comprehension support for your coursework.

In a language course where the learning outcome is writing/translation, you use AI/ MT to produce the assessed translation and submit it.

Why it’s not OK – it replaces the skill you’re meant to demonstrate.

Summarise weekly readings

Use
Acceptable
Unacceptable

Summarise weekly readings

You read each article and create your own study notes. You upload your notes into Claude and ask it to summarise your notes into a short paragraph.

Why it’s OK – you're still doing the reading and producing the notes yourself. The AI is helping you organise and simplify your own work, not someone else's.

You upload each required reading that you accessed through the Library into Claude and ask it to create a summary.

Why it’s not OK – readings accessed through the Library may be protected by copyright or license agreements which may be breached by uploading them to an external AI tool.
