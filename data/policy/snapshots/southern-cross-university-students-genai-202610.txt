Generative AI for Students in the Southern Cross Model

This resource provides students with information on the use of Generative AI (GenAI) within the Southern Cross Model.

Log on to Copilot using your SCU account

At Southern Cross University, we are committed to harnessing the transformative potential of Generative AI (GenAI) for our students, staff and academics.

As you learn in the Southern Cross Model, we want you to pilot, use, understand and critique GenAI, in ways that are authentic and integral to your professional careers. We will help you do that.

You will be exposed to GenAI in a variety of ways across your SCU journey, through teaching, assessment, feedback, research, and navigating your unit content.

At SCU, we take a “first principles approach” in recognising that GenAI is a software that is not inherently good or bad and is a tool that can be used constructively to enhance students' and academics' efficiencies in the way they learn and teach.

SCU has adopted a policy that supports and encourages the use of GenAI where it does not pose an unacceptable risk to the assurance of academic standards and integrity.

SCU believes in the benefits GenAI will produce for students and staff and has committed to providing an educative approach to mitigate the risks of using Gen AI tools in inappropriate or unethical ways.

What is Generative Artificial Intelligence?

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) refers to systems that can create new content such as text, images, audio, video, or computer code (Feuerriegel et al., 2024). These systems typically learn patterns from existing data and use them to generate outputs that appear novel and meaningful. For example, Large Language Models (LLMs) can produce human-like text, while diffusion models and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) can create realistic images, audio, or video.

Different types of GenAI serve different purposes:

- LLMs generate and process text or code.

- Diffusion models and GANs generate visual, audio, or multimedia content.

At SCU, students are encouraged to use only university-approved GenAI tools. This ensures that personal and institutional data are handled responsibly and not exposed to unauthorised use.

Where GenAI is Used

Education

The education world was thrown into chaos in 2022 when ChatGPT was first released (Quince, 2024). There was a lot of debate in all sectors of education about academic integrity, the future of graduates' capabilities and more. Most sectors banned the technology in the first instance. However, in 2025, most educational settings are now integrating GenAI in teaching and learning.

Creative Works

The rapid development of GenAI caused several issues with creative works, and still are in 2025. With GenAI tools a completely unique image can be generated in seconds. This creates questions around authorship, intellectual property and copyright.

Ethical Use of GenAI

Data Privacy and Security

While GenAI tools offer powerful ways to brainstorm, write, and research, they also introduce complex risks around data collection, ownership, security, and privacy. When you type something into a GenAI tool, that information doesn't necessarily disappear after you close the tab. GenAI systems often collect user inputs to improve their models.

Bias, Fairness, and Inclusivity

GenAI tools are trained on datasets pulled from the Internet and other places. As a result, they reflect the tone and information of the dataset which can create outputs that are biased or exclusionary. Bias, equity, accessibility and power are ethical implications discussed when using GenAI.

Transparency and Disclosure

Transparency in how GenAI tools are used is essential for maintaining trust, accountability, and integrity. As these tools become more commonplace, being clear about what was generated or assisted by GenAI is important.

How to acknowledge use of GenAI in your work

Professional Standards

As GenAI becomes more integrated into workplaces, industries are setting clear expectations for how these tools should be used. Whether studying to become a teacher, lawyer, nurse, engineer, artist, or business leader, it's important to understand how to ethically use GenAI.

Reminder: Always check the sources of information you find through GenAI to confirm that they are credible, reliable, authoritative, accurate and fit for purpose - i.e. is there bias representing a certain perspective on a matter?

Using GenAI Tools as a Student

We believe that developing skills in the responsible use of GenAI is an important part of your learning in the Southern Cross Model. There is a process for your Unit Assessor to communicate how you may or may not use tools for specific purposes. These are called the GenAI tool use descriptors. If you are not sure if you can or cannot use GenAI tools in your assessment, please reach out to your Unit Assessor. If in doubt, then do not use it until you have clarity. Your Unit Assessors will also communicate how you need to record your use of GenAI.

Tool Use Descriptors

The GenAI Tool Use Descriptors help clarify requirements for each assessment type, ranging from Full Use (where GenAI is embedded throughout the assessment), to Purpose-Specific Use (only allowed in certain tasks, as guided by your Unit Assessor), to No Use (typically for in-person assessments like exams or interviews). Your Unit Assessor will guide you on what's permitted, and it's essential to keep records of your GenAI prompts, outputs, and drafts. For support with correct referencing and attribution, visit the Library and the Learning Zone.

Common uses of GenAI tools

Critical Thinking and Academic Reading

- Break down complex texts into smaller summaries

- Highlight key points or generate discussion questions

- Clarify difficult terminology

- Explore different perspectives

- Test the strength of your reasoning and identify gaps

Drafting, Report Writing, and Academic Presentation (where permitted)

- Outline essays or reports

- Expand bullet points into full paragraphs

- Generate first drafts for refinement

- Improve clarity, coherence, and academic tone

- Reformat content into slides or speaking notes

Scheduling, Planning, and Supporting Learning

- Creating study or revision schedules

- Breaking down tasks into manageable steps

- Offering reminders or to-do lists

- Providing explanations of difficult topics

- Suggesting revision techniques tailored to you

Ideation and Creative Thinking

- Brainstorming essay topics or research angles

- Suggesting new directions for a project

- Recommending relevant concepts, sources, or examples

- Visualising ideas with mind maps or structured outlines

- Adapting ideas for different audiences or formats

Activities to try out

Activity 1: Academic Text Summariser

Goal: Summarise a reading, identify key ideas, or prepare for class discussion.

Step 1: Choose Your Own Article - Select a short article or abstract from your current coursework. It must be from a resource that allows educational use (open access journal or report found on the Internet)

Step 2: Generating an Output - Use the two prompts below to summarise your text.

- "Summarise this"

- "Summarise this article in 3 sentences, focusing on the research question, methods, and"

Activity 2: Essay Outline Builder

Goal: Develop a clear and structured plan for an academic essay or report (that is approved for GenAI use).

Step 1: Choose a Topic - Select a topic you are currently studying or plan to write about in an upcoming assignment.

Step 2: Generating an Output - Use the two prompts below to generate an outline.

"Create an outline for an essay about [your topic]."

"Create a structured essay outline for [your topic], including an introduction with a thesis statement, three main arguments with supporting evidence, and a conclusion."

Activity 3: Rewriting for Academic Tone

Goal: Improve clarity and academic style in written work.

Step 1: Choose a Paragraph - Write or copy a short paragraph (3–5 sentences) explaining a concept, argument, or finding related to your coursework (with permission or use an open source document).

Step 2: Generating an Output - Use the two prompts below to refine the writing.

"Rewrite this paragraph in academic language."

"Edit this paragraph to improve clarity, coherence, and academic tone. Avoid casual language and make it suitable for a university-level assignment."

Activity 4: Presentation Slide Scaffold

Goal: Convert written content into presentation slide scaffolds for visual communication.

Step 1: Choose Content - Use a short report, essay section, or detailed paragraph from your notes or a reading (with permission).

Step 2: Generating an Output - Use the two prompts below to transform the content into a presentation format.

"Turn this into a presentation."

"Convert this text into 5 PowerPoint slides, including a title slide and 4 content slides. Use clear headings and bullet points for each."

Activity 5: Study Planner Creation

Goal: Plan your time and manage upcoming deadlines effectively.

Step 1: List Your Tasks - Make a list of upcoming assignments, tests, and study goals for the next few weeks.

Step 2: Generating an Output - Use the two prompts below to create a schedule.

"Make a study plan."

"Based on these tasks and deadlines, create a weekly study schedule that balances reading, writing, revision, and rest over the next 3 weeks."
