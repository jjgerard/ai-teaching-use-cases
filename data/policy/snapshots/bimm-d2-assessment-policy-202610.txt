Introduction
This handbook is for all students at BIMM University. During your studies, you’ll be assessed in various
ways to help you understand your progress in your learning. Each course is assessed slightly
differently, so you must ensure you’ve read anything you’re given explaining how assessments will
work for you.
At BIMM University, we design all our assessments and the marking criteria used to assess you. This
might differ from what you’ve experienced before, where work is often set according to an exam
board syllabus or sent off to an exam board to be marked. Some modes of assessment you’ll have
come across before, such as performances, presentations, collaborative projects or class activities,
while others might be new to you.
You’ll also need to be familiar with the criteria you’re being judged against – we call them Assessment
Fields – understanding these will help you to prepare for assessments more effectively. This
information can be found in the Module Specifications, which will be on Canvas.
Broadly, there are two types of assessment on BIMM University courses as follows:
1. Coursework assessments are submitted online through the University’s VLE, Canvas, and are
assessed remotely by academic staff.
2. Face-to-face or practice-based assessments include performances, demonstrations,
presentations, and other activities that are assessed in person.
In addition, we have:
1. Formative assessments are ungraded assessments designed to support your learning by
providing feedback on the skills and knowledge you are developing.
2. Summative assessments are graded1 evaluations designed to assess your learning at the end
of a module and provide feedback on what you have done well and what you might improve
in the future.
3. Negotiated assessment2 – where you can propose how you will meet the learning outcomes
through assessment (proposals for negotiated assessment must be agreed with the Course or
Module Leader).

Examples of modes of assessment
Assessment modes may include:
•

Practical: A performance, educational or professional practice-based assessment.

•

Oral: An individual or group presentation, discussion, marketing/sales pitch, performance or
teaching exercise.

•

Portfolio: Normally, a series of tasks or creative artefacts collated as part of one assessment.

•

Artefact: A single piece of work, such as a visual, audio, software, composition, design or
artistic output.

•

Text-based: A report, essay, review, analysis, case study, creative or professional written

Assessment information
You must know why, how, and when you will be assessed during your course, as well as your
responsibilities as a BIMM University student. We will make all this information available to ensure
this is the case.
Your Course Leader will ensure you are informed of the assessment arrangements and
requirements at the start of each semester/trimester. They will also provide the dates, times, and
venues for the practical assessments. Your complete assessment timetable will be published at
least two weeks before the first assessment.
You should keep a record of the dates, times, and venues for your assessments , including
Retrievals (opportunities to retake an assessment if you fail on the first attempt). Check the
details of your timetable and flag any possible clashes or omissions with your Course Leader.
You must be available throughout the academic year, including Retrieval periods (available here).
You should not make any holiday plans until the Assessment Boards have confirmed marks, after
which you will normally be given at least two weeks’ notice of any Retrieval assessments.
The critical information for each module you study is contained in the Module Specification ,
which sets out the following assessment details:
•

The Learning Outcomes for the module and each assessment are statements that describe
what you need to be able to do to pass the module.

•

A description of what the assessment entails, the week it is due in each semester/trimester,
how to submit, present, or attend the assessment, and any required elements or subcomponents.

•

The mode of the assessment, re-assessment, and the Retrieval assessment in case you fail on
your first attempt.

•

The criteria (Assessment Fields against which you are assessed for each assessment).

•

If your module has more than one summative (graded) assessment, each assessment will
have a percentage weighting that indicates its relative size and/or importance. These
weightings will add up to 100% and will be used to calculate your final module mark. For
example, if Assessment 1 is 30% and Assessment 2 is 70%, a single grade will be assigned to
each assessment. If Assessment 1 is assigned a mark of 55% and Assessment 2 a mark of 68%,
and both marks are combined in their weighted proportions, they produ ce a module mark of
64.1%, which would be rounded to a final module grade of 64%.

•

Your modules may also include formative assessments labelled Assessment Zero or A0. These
assessments are designed to help you develop the skills required by the module and to
provide you with feedback on your learning before you undertake graded summative
assessments.

Your Course Leaders will also ensure that you are aware of the University’s Academic Assessment
Regulations for undergraduate and postgraduate courses, as appropriate, and of the policies on
Exceptional Circumstances and Academic Integrity. In particular, you should be made aware of
any penalties for late submission of assessments, the importance of academic integrity, and your
right to appeal marks in certain circumstances.
We do not apply any penalty if you submit coursework up to 24 hours late, including Retrievals.
This is because we understand that you may sometimes miss a deadline, however hard you try.

If you submit coursework more than 24 hours late, a penalty will apply (see the regulations for

If you fail to attend a practical assessment or submit coursework, a mark of zero will be recorded.
If these marks result in the module being failed, you will be asked to take a Retrieval assessment
(see the assessment regulations links above).
All material submitted for assessment must be your own (except where group work is specifically
part of the assessment); see our Academic Integrity Policy. In addition, all quotations and content
from other people's or organisations' published or unpublished work must be appropriately
attributed, both at the relevant point in the text and in the bibliography or reference list. Study
skills resources to help you understand these requirements are available here. In addition, the
use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in the work you submit for assessment must comply with the
guidelines set out in our Academic Integrity Policy and be fully attributed.
If other circumstances (such as a disability or another long-term condition) affect your ability to
complete assessments, you should consult the Exceptional Circumstances Policy.
If you submit a recording, composition, or lyrics not in English, a translation must also be provided
for marking and moderation purposes.

Unless otherwise specified in the Module Specification, coursework submissions should comply
with these guidelines:
•

If your coursework draws on external sources (text or otherwise), you should include a

available here.

Your electronic data files must be in the correct format and comply with file size
requirements, as set out in the relevant Module Specification.

When you submit a file, you will be asked to confirm that you wish to upload it and to affirm
that the work is your own.

•

It is your responsibility to ensure that you submit the correct file, in a valid format, within any
specified file size limits, by the deadline, and to the correct submission point published on
Canvas.

•

If you submit a file that does not meet the requirements listed above and the deadline has
not yet passed, the file may be resubmitted.

If the deadline has passed and a resubmission has been made, the original file will be marked ,
and a lateness penalty may be applied. Depending on what was submitted, when, and
whether the Marker can open it, this may result in a low or fail mark.

•

To avoid losing your work, you should back up all digitally stored work (we recommend doing
this at least twice on different media).

In addition, text-based submissions should follow the following guidelines:

A cover sheet containing the student number, module name, assessment number/title and

Assessment size limits and marking
The purpose of an assessment size limit is to give all students across the University a clear indication
of the maximum size of a piece of assessed work, the amount of work expected, and how they should
allocate time to one piece of assessed work in relation to others. Working to set limits is a practical
and academic skill required within the creative industries.
Our policy is that assessment briefs must include an assessment size or time limit, as appropriate, and
it must be made clear whether any required elements of the assessment fall within or outside this
limit.
There is no prescribed penalty for exceeding the assessment limits. However, staff are allocated a set
amount of time to mark each assessment and are under no obligation to assess work that exceeds the
limits. Faculty will use their discretion, but work that is considerably over-length will be disregarded,
and work that is substantially under-length is unlikely to address the brief fully. Therefore, in both
cases, you are likely to lose marks. Moreover, if working to a strict limit is a critical element of the
brief, markers will always deduct marks if the work is too long or too short.

Oral & practical assessments
Oral and practical assessments will be conducted by faculty from the subject area. You may bring only
authorised materials to oral or practical assessments. Possessing or using unauthorised materials
during an assessment may infringe the University’s Academic Integrity Policy. If in any doubt, discuss
with faculty staff what you can and cannot bring.
Students are not permitted to record oral or practical assessments, but faculty will record these
assessments for Internal and External Moderation. You may request permission to copy specific
recordings from performance-based assessments for inclusion in your showreel, and the Head of
School will process any such requests. Video material will be released only once the module has been
marked and passed.
All face-to-face/in-person assessments will be supervised by the faculty member conducting the
assessment. Any incidents or abnormalities during an assessment will be recorded and reported to the
Course Leader.
You must arrive in good time for practical and oral assessments and are advised to be there 15 minutes
before the assessment commences. The assessor may allow late arrivals to undertake the assessment if
the assessment schedule permits. However, if a late student cannot be accommodated and you arrive
very late (30 minutes or more), you will be considered to have missed the assessment submission
opportunity, and a zero mark will be recorded. You will be able to present evidence in mitigation of late
arrival for consideration by the Mitigating Evidence Committee.

The BIMM University Assessment Rubric
A rubric is a tool that helps standardise the grading process, making it more efficient, saving time for
faculty and providing you with effective feedback that promotes your learning. Rubrics set out
appropriate criteria and describe them across a range of performance levels. The BIMM University
Assessment Rubric recognises the need for an assessment approach applicable to the creative arts.
Accordingly, the design of our rubric draws on six specific Assessment Fields:
•

Investigation.

•

Knowledge.

•

Development.

•

Communication.

•

Production.

•

Technical.

These six Assessment Fields measure achievement based on the quality of evidence in your assessed
work.
Furthermore, we use these fields selectively for each assessment task, making them applicable to
different assessment modes across our provision (most assessments will be marked using three to
four fields). Marks will be awarded holistically, with each assessment given a single mark using the
University’s categorical marking scheme (see pages 13 and 14), calculated using the rubric to make a
grading judgment. Where there is more than one assessment on a module, marks are combined by
their relative weighting to provide a module mark as described above.

How do I know that my marks are fair?
All the assessments you complete in your course should be conducted rigorously. Faculty are provided
with procedures that must be strictly followed, ensuring that students are treated fairly. All
assessments are graded using the University’s Assessment Rubric, which serves as a reference for all
markers and Internal/External Moderators (External Examiners – academic staff from other
universities with no connection to BIMM University). You should read the assessment brief and the
descriptions of each assessment field before you complete the assessment to understand what is
expected of you. If there is anything you do not understand, you should ask your lecturer or your
Course Leader.
We also have a series of regulations that all staff must adhere to, governing how assessments are
developed and delivered. These regulations outline the following:
•

How the assessments are designed.

•

The application of the University assessment rubric to all assessments.

•

How the marking process is undertaken.

•

How the procedures for calibration and moderation are conducted (see below on page 16).

Panel marking
Some of our assessments, especially performance and practical ones, are marked by a panel.
Panel-marking processes follow established procedures and include discussions about the Assessment
Rubric and its application to that particular assessment. This helps ensure that your work is assessed
consistently and fairly, and that any markers’ personal preferences don’t affect the mark you receive.

Calibration and Moderation
To ensure that assessment criteria are applied consistently by markers, that students are treated fairly
throughout the assessment process, and that there is a shared understanding of the academic
standards students are expected to achieve, we deploy three strategies:
1) Calibration – a process that ensures standards are understood by faculty and consistently
applied. We use examples of previous work to align all markers on a module, and faculty agree
on the standards they are working to, so they can tell you what ‘good’, ‘outstanding’, and
‘exceptional’ look like in the work you produce.
2) Internal Moderation – a process that checks that standards are applied consistently at the
module level, within or across campuses. We ask markers who did not mark the work initially
to review the consistency of colleagues' marking by examining student work samples.
3) External Moderation – this process is undertaken by External Examiners who work at other
universities and are experts in the subject area you are studying. They review work from across
all campuses and compare standards with those they are familiar with across the sector.
External Examiners can raise any concerns they have about marking in their annual reports.

Internal Moderation and Double Marking
17

At Level 3 (foundation years) and 4 (the first year of an undergraduate degree), all your work is marked
pass/fail, and we use calibration to ensure that marking is fair and consistent. New faculty members
cannot mark your work until they have completed calibration training.
At all other levels (Levels 5, 6 and 7), Internal and External Moderation is used alongside calibration
to ensure standards are applied fairly and consistently. In addition, assessed work on any doubleweighted modules (30-credit modules for undergraduate courses and 60-credit modules for
postgraduate courses) is marked independently by two markers, who then meet to agree on a mark.
We call this double-marking.
The procedure for the Internal Moderation of assessments is as follows:
•

The First Marker grades the student's work using the University Assessment Rubric and
Categorical Marking Scheme, annotates the work as necessary, and provides you with feedback
on how your mark has been determined.

•

The Moderator reviews a percentage of work to ensure that the Assessment Rubric and
Categorical Marking Scheme have been applied consistently and at the correct pitch by the First
Marker(s), and to evaluate the quality of feedback.

•

If the Moderator identifies a problem with the consistency of marks awarded by a First Marker,
the Course Leader is informed, all the First Marker’s assessments are double-marked, and the new
marks are agreed.

•

If the Moderator identifies a problem with the quality of feedback from a First Marker, the
Moderator reports it to the Course Leader, who then works with that First Marker to revisit and
improve their feedback.

•

If the Moderator identifies a problem with the pitch (i.e., consistent over- or under-marking), the
Moderator will report it to the Course Leader, who will then report it to the Deputy Chair of the
Assessment Board. A recalibration will be agreed in consultation with the External Examiner.

•

Once the Internal Moderation/double-marking process is complete, External Moderation is
carried out by External Examiners.

The procedure for double-marking assessments is as follows:
•

The guidance we provide to our staff on marking the work of
students with Specific Learning Difficulties.
Understanding the nature of students' disabilities and how they may affect students' responses to
assessments informs an inclusive approach to marking. Students with disabilities have a legal right to
additional support and reasonable adjustments. This is achieved by providing an Individual Support
Plan (ISP), prepared in consultation with your Course Leader and Student Life and shared with relevant
teaching and administrative staff. The ISP will detail agreed adjustments to delivery and assessment
and make recommendations to teaching staff on strategies that may be deployed to support students
with Specific Learning Difficulties (SpLDs).
Even if a student does not have a formal diagnosis, we will still provide an Individual Support Plan if
we have knowledge that disability-related adjustments would support the student. This aligns with
universities' legal responsibilities, including those of teaching staff, as outlined in the Abrahart v Bristol
decision. The court held that a university need not have formal knowledge of a disability and its effects
to owe a duty to make reasonable adjustments. Provided it has 'constructive knowledge' of the effects
of the condition (for example, through the student's behaviour or language), it should be able to
deduce that a student has a disability. The decision also said that student-facing staff cannot pass on
the duty to consider reasonable adjustments to a disability or wellbeing Service but must consider
what proactive steps they should take in a timely way to ensure the student is not disadvantaged
because of their disability. For more information, see the Equality and Human Rights Commission's
advice note for the higher education sector on this case.
A specific learning difficulty (SpLD) is a difference or difficulty in particular aspects of learning. The
most common SpLDs are Dyslexia, Dyspraxia, Attention Deficit Disorder/Attention Deficit
(Hyperactivity) Disorder, Dyscalculia and Dysgraphia. However, sometimes a diagnosis of Specific
Learning Difficulties is given rather than Dyslexia or Dyspraxia, because the individual experiences a
unique combination of difficulties that cannot be readily categorised but may include features of one
or more recognised SpLDs. Similarly, some people will have an assessment for more than one SpLD;
overlap is very common.
SpLDs are lifelong neurological differences that can present barriers to new learning, recalling,
processing information, and literacy acquisition. A guide to common neurodivergent types can be
found here.
SpLD traits can present themselves in many positive ways, such as:
•

Above-average intelligence.

•

Exceptional creativity.

•

Unique problem-solving skills.

•

A keen sense of empathy and justice.

•

Good intuition.

However, someone living with an SpLD may also experience the following:
•

Poor focus and attention.

•

Poor handwriting.

Our university Assessment Rubric sets out the knowledge and skills that must be demonstrated for
grades to be awarded. The Assessment Rubric includes an assessment field that focuses on the
technical aspects of work submitted for assessment. When this field applies to text-based work,
markers should look for punctuation, spelling, and grammar errors and point them out to students in
feedback. Students should also be encouraged to use spelling and grammar checkers when
proofreading their work before submission. Markers may deduct marks where it is clear that students
have not taken sufficient care over the technical aspects of their written work, particularly when such
errors interfere with the reader's ability to understand the text.
However, when marking the work of students with SpLDs, markers should be aware that SpLD
students’ written assignments may lack the polished writing demonstrated by their peers due to
difficulties with spelling, grammar, and sentence structure. Errors may persist despite extensive
proofreading. In normal circumstances, markers might reasonably consider such work technically
flawed or careless and deduct marks for poor presentation. However, in the case of SpLD students,
such errors should be understood and taken into account when assessing written work.
Consider SpLD when marking:
•

Read the work and look for ideas, understanding, and knowledge.

•

Be clear about the marking criteria (rubric), especially knowledge and understanding.

•

Make constructive and positive comments.

•

Explain your comments in a straightforward and accessible way.

What sort of feedback will I get?
You’ll receive plenty of feedback during your studies, which should help you learn and develop.
Receiving both critical and positive comments is entirely normal and isn’t a sign of weakness. Everyone
will receive both positive and critical feedback.
Whatever your course, you’re being prepared to enter a highly demanding profession in which you’ll
be expected to take constructive criticism and use it to improve your approach. Responding well to
constructive feedback will help you meet professional expectations of behaviour.
There are three main types of feedback that we use:
1. Informal, iterative feedback from your teacher and possibly from your peers in class and
during online sessions.
2. Formative assessment feedback is more formal feedback from your teachers and peers,
designed to help you improve a specific piece of work for assessment.
3. Summative assessment feedback is the feedback you receive on formal, graded ‘summative'
assessments.
You’ll receive a range of feedback during your time at BIMM University. You'll be given feedback
whenever you complete an assessment, and you’ll also often receive feedback to help you improve,
even when you haven’t completed a specific assessment. Most of your feedback will come from your
lecturers, but you’ll also get feedback from your classmates. Don’t underestimate the value of your
peers as a source of information. Sharing experiences within your class can be worthwhile. From time
to time, you might be asked to comment on your work, too.
Sometimes, you’ll get your feedback straight away. For instance, if you perform for your classmates in
class, they might be asked to give you feedback during the lesson.
Sometimes, giving feedback is a bit more complicated – different people will mark some of the
assessments you do to ensure that you have all been marked fairly. Making sure that marking is
consistent and that feedback is helpful can take a little while, but comments on assessed work should
always be back to you promptly, which means within 21 days (plus any public holidays) of you
submitting it. However, if we fail to meet this deadline, we will contact you to explain why your
feedback is late and when you will receive it.

Forms of feedback
•

Written feedback is the most ubiquitous format and effectively supports several types of
assessment, particularly text-based assignments, where markers may also provide annotated
comments. It can be produced quickly, is easy to distribute, and can be sent electronically due to
its small file size. It may be printed out and read in hard copy. However, it can be seen as overly
formal, less substantial, and less detailed, and it may not suit all markers or all students.

•

Audio feedback is becoming increasingly popular. It may be faster to produce (with practice),
though it may be slower to distribute due to larger file sizes, and it requires the ability to use audio
recording technology. It also has advantages in that it can be perceived as more personal, and
tone and emphasis provide improved student understanding. In addition, it is more easily
understandable and provides much more detail than written feedback.

•

Video feedback is much more demanding for markers in terms of technology and can be slow to
record and distribute because of large file sizes. However, this format is highly engaging, can be
extremely sophisticated (including multimedia input), provides all the advantages of audio
feedback, is perceived as dynamic, and offers students greater insight into their performance.

•

Generic or group feedback – with some assessments, it can be beneficial to provide this form of
feedback to a class, either alongside or ahead of individual feedback, as it allows faculty to have a
dialogue with students based on some of the generic themes emerging from their collective work.

•

Peer feedback – peer assessment and feedback can effectively engage students in the process
and in understanding how assessment and feedback support learning.

How do I make the best use of feedback?
There’s no point working hard for an assessment only to not get the most out of the comments
you’re given. So here are some steps for using your feedback effectively:
1. Knowing what feedback you expect, you can better prepare for it.
2. Will it be formal or informal?
3. Written or verbal?
4. Online or in person?
5. If you’re not sure what to expect, please ask.

Be prepared
It sounds obvious, but most informal, iterative feedback happens in class – make sure you’re there
and ready to receive it:
•

Before receiving formative or summative feedback, check the relevant Assessment Fields for your
assessment to see how your work is being evaluated. If you’re unsure of the criteria, check Canvas
or ask your teacher.

•

On many BIMM University courses, there’s minimal (if any) automated marking – whether
iterative, formative or summative, feedback is usually provided by a person, not a computer. That
means you need to find out who to get feedback from, whether they work full‑ or part‑time, and
how to book a feedback session in advance. Check how it’s best to ask questions in class, via
Canvas, or by email.

•

Think about how you’ll keep track of your feedback when you receive it, so you have a record to
reflect on later and don’t rely on your memory. For example, you could write notes in a notebook
or record the class on your mobile phone or another recording device.

Understanding feedback
•

If you’re receiving verbal or audio feedback, listen to the comments you’re given. Ask for
clarification if you can’t hear them clearly or don’t understand. Make sure you know how you can
improve next time.

•

If you receive written feedback, read the comments, ensure you understand them, and carefully
consider how they apply to your work.

•

When you receive formative or summative feedback, consider how the marking criteria have
contributed to the mark or grade you have been given. Understanding what’s gone well and what
you need to work on will help you improve and should lead to better grades in the future.

•

Some teachers run feedback sessions or tutorials. These are a beneficial way to get formative
feedback, so take advantage of them by coming along with some specific questions.

25

Using feedback to learn
•

When you’re given informal, iterative feedback in class, try to put the comments into practice
straight away.

•

When you receive formative or summative feedback, consider how you would redo the work (or
parts of it) based on the comments you’ve been given, and how you’d approach a similar piece of
work differently having had that feedback.

•

Always identify what you’ve done well and what you need to do to improve.

•

Think about how you can apply the feedback you’ve received in other situations. For example, you
should use the iterative and formative feedback you receive in class to help you prepare for future
assessments.

•

If any particular areas need improvement (such as your writing style or referencing), additional
support is available – please ask your teacher or Course Leader.

Glossary
Assessment: A way of measuring students’ skills and/or understanding.
External Examiner: Someone from another institution who ensures the course is run fairly and
rigorously.
Informal iterative feedback: Informal, frequent feedback in which you’re asked to reflect on the
comments your teacher or peers give you.
Formative feedback: Formal or informal feedback that helps you understand what you need to do to
improve your assessment performance; it may or may not be graded.
Summative feedback: Formal feedback you’ll receive after completing an assessment to show how
well you’re progressing; it will usually include a grade or a pass/fail for Level 4.
Assessment Rubric: The criteria used by tutors to determine the mark you should be awarded for a
piece of work.
Moderator: Someone who ensures that marks are consistently awarded across an
assessment/module/course.
Module Specification: The document that tells you about the module you’re taking – you can find it
on Canvas.
Tutorial: A session with a teacher in which you can review your past work in detail.
