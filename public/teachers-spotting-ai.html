Why this may not match what you see when marking

My reading of the evidence, not established findings.

You judge your own students

These studies are cold judgements. Only Kurt et al. let teachers use what they knew about the course and students, and that was three teachers. A baseline of a student's earlier writing has not been tested with teachers. One algorithm that compares against it (Oliveira et al. 2025) is promising.

Default output is easier than prompted output

Fleckenstein's AI essays were prompted to be weak or medium. When ChatGPT was asked to make student-like grammar mistakes, instructors scored 54% (Saarna 2024). Frequent LLM users (Russell et al.) know the default vocabulary and structure.

You hear about the ones you catch

In the exam study, markers flagged 4 of 63 AI scripts and had no way to learn about the other 59. Without feedback, confidence stays high at low accuracy.

Catching and clearing differ

Experienced teachers cleared 73% of real student essays but caught 38% of AI ones. "It's usually obvious" fits that pattern: the AI you spot is obvious, and the rest passes as student work.

Exposure matters more than teaching years

Frequent LLM users reached 93% while non-experts hit chance. Linguistics experts (Casal & Kessler) got 38.9%, and self-reported ChatGPT experience did not help in Waltzer's studies.

The target is moving

Persona prompts, style imitation and humanizers weaken the cues. I found no human-detection study on the newest models.

Gaps

  • No experiment gives teachers a student's earlier writing and tests whether that helps.
  • No experiment with primary-school teachers, and none with teachers who know the pupils.
  • No pooled estimate for teachers; the one review aimed at this question (Han 2025) I could only read in abstract.
  • No larger replication of the Russell et al. result, and no controlled study of teacher training with feedback.
  • No data on how many proven misconduct cases came from human suspicion rather than software.
